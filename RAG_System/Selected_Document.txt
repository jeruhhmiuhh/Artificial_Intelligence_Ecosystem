Tarot is a pack of playing cards, used from the mid-15th century in various parts of Europe to play games such as Italian tarocchini, French tarot, and Austrian Königrufen. Many of these games are still played today. In the late 18th century, some tarot decks began to be used for divination purposes via tarot card reading and cartomancy.

Like common playing cards, the tarot has four suits which vary by region: French suits in Northern Europe, Latin suits in Southern Europe, and German suits in Central Europe. Each suit has 14 cards: ten pip cards numbering from one (or Ace) to ten and four face cards (King, Queen, Knight, and Jack/Knave). In addition, the tarot has a separate 22-card section known as the Major Arcana (or Trump cards). The Fool is usually a separate card numbered 0 and the other 21 cards are numbered 1 through 21.

The tarot originated in northern Italy during the 15th century. The oldest surviving tarot cards are the 15 or so Visconti-Sforza decks painted in the mid-15th century for the rulers of the Duchy of Milan. The names and symbols of the trump cards reflect medieval Christian culture and imagery. Before the 18th century, tarot cards were not associated with mysticism, magic, or divination.

The 22 Major Arcana cards carry deep symbolic meanings. Card 0, The Fool, represents new beginnings, freedom, and a leap of faith. Card 1, The Magician, symbolizes willpower, skill, and resourcefulness. Card 2, The High Priestess, stands for intuition, mystery, and inner knowledge. Card 3, The Empress, embodies fertility, abundance, and the nurturing of nature. Card 4, The Emperor, denotes authority, structure, and fatherly protection. Card 5, The Hierophant, represents tradition, institutions, and spiritual guidance. Card 6, The Lovers, signifies relationships, choices, and alignment of values. Card 7, The Chariot, reflects determination, control, and victory. Card 8, Strength, conveys courage, patience, and inner power. Card 9, The Hermit, points to solitude, soul-searching, and inner wisdom. Card 10, The Wheel of Fortune, represents cycles, karma, and destiny. Card 11, Justice, stands for fairness, truth, and cause and effect. Card 12, The Hanged Man, denotes suspension, sacrifice, and a new perspective. Card 13, Death, symbolizes endings, transitions, and transformation. Card 14, Temperance, reflects balance, moderation, and purpose. Card 15, The Devil, represents shadow self, addiction, and materialism. Card 16, The Tower, signifies sudden change, upheaval, and revelation. Card 17, The Star, conveys hope, renewal, and spiritual connection. Card 18, The Moon, embodies illusion, fear, and the unconscious. Card 19, The Sun, represents positivity, vitality, and success. Card 20, Judgement, signifies reflection, reckoning, and absolution. Card 21, The World, stands for completion, integration, and accomplishment.

The Minor Arcana consists of 56 cards divided into four suits: Wands, Cups, Swords, and Pentacles (also known as Coins or Disks). The suit of Wands is associated with the element of Fire and represents creativity, ambition, passion, and career. The suit of Cups is associated with the element of Water and corresponds to emotions, relationships, intuition, and the inner world. The suit of Swords is linked to the element of Air and governs intellect, conflict, truth, and communication. The suit of Pentacles is connected to the element of Earth and deals with the material world, finances, health, and practical matters.

Each suit in the Minor Arcana contains four court cards: the Page, Knight, Queen, and King. Court cards often represent people in the querent's life or aspects of their own personality. Pages typically represent young people or the beginning of a new journey in the suit's domain. Knights embody action and movement — rushing forward or sometimes recklessly charging ahead. Queens represent mastery of the internal, emotional aspects of their suits. Kings represent mastery of the external, practical expression of their suits.

A tarot reading involves a reader laying out cards in a pattern called a spread. Each position in the spread has a predetermined meaning, and the card occupying that position is interpreted accordingly. The most famous spread is the Celtic Cross, a ten-card layout that explores the past, present, possible future, internal and external influences, hopes, and fears around a central question. A simple three-card spread can represent past, present, and future, or alternatively situation, action, and outcome.

Reversed cards, also called inversions, occur when a card is drawn upside down. Many readers interpret reversed cards as representing blocked or internalized energy, delays, or a need to look inward. Other readers choose to read all cards in the upright position.

Numerology plays an important role in tarot. The Aces represent new beginnings and raw potential. The Twos signify balance, duality, and partnership. Threes embody creativity, expansion, and initial growth. Fours relate to stability, structure, and consolidation. Fives bring change, conflict, and instability. Sixes represent harmony, reconciliation, and equilibrium. Sevens relate to reflection, assessment, and spiritual development. Eights convey movement, power, and momentum. Nines signify near-completion, fulfillment, and culmination. Tens represent completion, transition, and the end of a cycle.

Astrological associations are widely used in tarot interpretation. The Major Arcana cards each correspond to a zodiac sign or planet. The Fool is associated with Uranus or sometimes the element of Air. The Magician corresponds to Mercury. The High Priestess is linked to the Moon. The Empress corresponds to Venus. The Emperor is associated with Aries. The Hierophant corresponds to Taurus. The Lovers are linked to Gemini. The Chariot corresponds to Cancer. Strength is associated with Leo. The Hermit corresponds to Virgo. The Wheel of Fortune is linked to Jupiter. Justice corresponds to Libra. The Hanged Man is associated with Neptune. Death corresponds to Scorpio. Temperance is linked to Sagittarius. The Devil corresponds to Capricorn. The Tower is associated with Mars. The Star corresponds to Aquarius. The Moon is linked to Pisces. The Sun corresponds to the Sun itself. Judgement is associated with Pluto. The World is linked to Saturn.

The Rider-Waite-Smith tarot deck, published in 1909, is the most influential and widely used tarot deck in the English-speaking world. It was designed by occultist Arthur Edward Waite and illustrated by Pamela Colman Smith, both members of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn. For the first time, the pip cards of the Minor Arcana were illustrated with full scenes depicting people and activities, which made intuitive reading much more accessible. Before this deck, most tarot decks had non-scenic pip cards similar to regular playing cards.

The Thoth Tarot, created by occultist Aleister Crowley and painted by Lady Frieda Harris between 1938 and 1943, is the second most influential deck in the Western esoteric tradition. It incorporates Thelemic philosophy, astrology, Qabalah, and other elements of Crowley's magickal system. The geometric art of the pip cards was inspired by projective geometry.

Tarot became associated with occultism and esotericism in the late 18th century. Antoine Court de Gébelin, a French freemason and author, claimed in 1781 that the tarot contained hidden wisdom from ancient Egypt, a theory now known to be false but which had enormous influence on how the tarot was subsequently interpreted. Etteilla, the pen name of Jean-Baptiste Alliette, became the first professional tarot card reader around 1785. He also created the first tarot deck specifically designed for occult purposes.

In the 19th century, French occultists such as Eliphas Lévi further developed the association of tarot with the Kabbalah, astrology, and ceremonial magic. Lévi connected the 22 Major Arcana with the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet, a linkage that became foundational in later Western esotericism. The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, founded in London in 1888, systematized the occult interpretation of tarot and integrated it with the Tree of Life from the Kabbalah.

Chakra associations with tarot have become popular in modern spiritual practice. The Root Chakra (Muladhara), associated with grounding and survival, corresponds to the suit of Pentacles and earth energy. The Sacral Chakra (Svadhisthana), linked to creativity and emotion, resonates with the suit of Cups and water energy. The Solar Plexus Chakra (Manipura), connected to personal power and will, aligns with fire energy and the suit of Wands. The Heart Chakra (Anahata), the center of love and compassion, bridges the lower and upper chakras and resonates with the Lovers card. The Throat Chakra (Vishuddha), governing communication and truth, connects with the suit of Swords and air energy. The Third Eye Chakra (Ajna), associated with intuition and insight, aligns with The High Priestess and The Moon. The Crown Chakra (Sahasrara), representing spiritual connection and cosmic consciousness, resonates with The World and The Star.

Common tarot reading techniques include journaling after each reading to track patterns over time, pulling a daily card for reflection, and using tarot for meditation by sitting with a card and contemplating its imagery. Shadow work with tarot involves deliberately exploring uncomfortable cards to uncover unconscious patterns and suppressed emotions. Dream journaling alongside tarot readings can deepen understanding, as both modalities access the symbolic language of the unconscious mind.

Ethical considerations in professional tarot reading include avoiding predictions of death or specific medical diagnoses, emphasizing the querent's free will, maintaining confidentiality, and framing insights as possibilities rather than certainties. Responsible readers acknowledge that tarot is a tool for reflection, not a supernatural oracle with guaranteed accuracy.
